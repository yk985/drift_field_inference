{\large\color{accent} Six estimators, one set of walkers, and where each one wins}\\[0.8em]
{\color{ink3}\small The \code{dfi} package \quad\textbullet\quad 46 tests (45 pass, 1 skips) \quad\textbullet\quad
every number reproduced by a numbered script}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.2em}\hrule height 0.4pt \vspace{0.8em}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{The question.} Particles move by $\mathrm dx = b(x)\,\mathrm dt+\sqrt{2D}\,\mathrm dW$.
From their observed motion, recover the vector field $b$. The instantaneous signal-to-noise
ratio is about $10^{-3}$: over one step the drift contributes $b\dt$ and the noise
$\sqrt{2D\dt}$, so every method is an exercise in averaging away a thousandfold larger
random term without averaging away the field.

\textbf{What was built.} A simulator, six estimators of increasing structure, and one
measurement protocol that runs all of them on the \emph{same} walkers, so that differences
are differences between methods and not between experiments.

\textbf{The three results worth the reader's time.} (i) A network trained across many
simulated fields beats every classical estimator by ${\sim}40\%$ \textemdash{} and loses to
the simplest kernel on fields from a generator it never saw. (ii) At equilibrium,
\emph{static positions} carry more about the drift than the increments do, decisively so in
5D. (iii) Static positions are \emph{exactly} blind to circulation, which turns the pair of
families into a measurement of entropy production.
\end{keybox}

% ==========================================================================
\section{The problem, and why it splits in two}

The overdamped Langevin equation $\mathrm dx = b(x)\,\mathrm dt + \sqrt{2D}\,\mathrm dW$
is the standard model for a particle in a force field with thermal agitation, and its
inverse problem \textemdash{} recover $b$ from observed motion \textemdash{} appears in
single-particle tracking, active matter, ecology, and anywhere a stochastic
differential equation is fitted to a trajectory. Two kinds of data support two
fundamentally different answers.

\textbf{Trajectories.} Positions with time order, sampled at spacing $\dt$. The
Kramers\textendash Moyal relation makes the whole drift a conditional average,
\begin{equation}
  b(x) \;=\; \lim_{\dt\to0}\frac{1}{\dt}\,\mathbb E\big[\dx \,\big|\, x\big],
  \qquad\text{so}\qquad
  y_i \;\equiv\;\frac{\dx_i}{\dt} \;=\; b(x_i) + \text{noise of size}\ \sqrt{2D/\dt}.
  \label{eq:km}
\end{equation}
Every increment is an unbiased but violently noisy sample of the field at the point it
started from. Estimating $b$ is a regression of $y$ on $x$ in which the noise dominates
the signal by three orders of magnitude, and the six estimators below differ only in how
they pool neighbouring samples.

\textbf{Snapshots.} Positions \emph{without} time order, drawn from the stationary
density $\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}$. Here the only accessible object is the score:
\begin{equation}
  \nabla\log\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}(x) \;=\; -\nabla U(x)/D \;=\; b_{\mathrm{grad}}(x)/D .
  \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/helmholtz.png}
\caption{A drift field splits into a gradient part and a divergence-free rotational part,
$b=-\nabla U+\omega A\nabla U$. The rotational part carries a steady probability current
but leaves $\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}\propto e^{-U/D}$ \emph{exactly} unchanged \textemdash{} so a
snapshot dataset is identical in distribution for a system with no circulation and one
with a great deal of it.}
\end{figure}

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{The identifiability gap is not a weakness of any method.} Snapshots can recover
the gradient part and nothing else, whatever the estimator; trajectories can recover all
of $b$. This project measures that boundary rather than arguing about it, and then uses
it: the difference between what the two families see is the part of the dynamics that
breaks detailed balance.
\end{warnbox}

\section{Setup and measurement protocol}

\textbf{Fields.} Gaussian random fields on the torus $[-1,1]^d$ with a tunable
correlation length, decomposed as $b=-\nabla U+\omega A\nabla U$ with $A$ antisymmetric,
so $\omega$ dials circulation without touching $\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}$. Four other generators
(sparse wells, cellular patterns, mixed spectra, spatially varying anisotropy) exist for
out-of-distribution tests.

\textbf{Simulation.} Euler\textendash Maruyama with sub-stepping: the integrator runs at
$\dt/\text{substeps}$ while the data are recorded at $\dt$. Without that separation an
$O(\dt)$ bias in an estimator is indistinguishable from an $O(\dt)$ error in the
simulator, and one would be measuring one's own integrator. Trajectories are cached by
configuration hash, so every estimator sees bit-identical walkers.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{figs/trajectories.png}
\caption{What the estimators are given: 2000 walkers $\times$ 4000 steps in the same
field, without and with circulation ($\omega=0$ and $1.5$), paths coloured by time. The
drift is invisible in any single step \textemdash{} it contributes $b\dt\approx10^{-3}$ of
displacement against a random $\sqrt{2D\dt}\approx2.5\times10^{-2}$ \textemdash{} and
appears only after averaging thousands of them. The circulation on the right is likewise
not visible by eye in the paths, but it is recoverable from their increments and provably
absent from their positions.}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Score.} Normalised RMS error against the true field on a dense grid,
$\mathrm{nrmse}=\lVert\hat b-b\rVert/\lVert b\rVert$, so 1.0 means ``as bad as predicting
zero''. Reported alongside: coverage (what fraction of the box a method will answer for),
the cosine of the angle, the scale ratio, and the error split into gradient and rotational
components. Every method is scored on \emph{all} evaluation points, including ones it
declines to answer for \textemdash{} scoring only where a method claims support rewards
abstention.

\textbf{Tuning.} Every method chooses its own smoothing by cross-validation grouped
\emph{by walker}, never by sample. Splitting increments at random lets a held-out point sit
one time step away from its training neighbours, and the criterion then rewards a
bandwidth far too small. This bit one estimator in the study (§\ref{sec:selftuning}).

\section{The ladder}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}rL{0.20\textwidth}L{0.24\textwidth}L{0.40\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
& \textbf{method} & \textbf{consumes} & \textbf{what it assumes} \\
\midrule
1 & binned Kramers\textendash Moyal & increments & $b$ is constant inside a cell \\
2 & kernel regression (NW, local-linear) & increments & $b$ is smooth on one length scale \\
3 & basis projection (SFI) & increments & $b$ lies in a Fourier basis \\
4 & neural network (MLP, CNN) & increments & smoothness implied by architecture + early stopping \\
4b & amortised CNN & increments, binned to a grid & \textbf{what fields from this generator look like} \\
5 & KDE score, denoising score matching & positions only, plus $D$ & \textbf{equilibrium} (gradient drift) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Rungs 1\textendash 3: hand-built smoothing.} Binning averages increments per cell
and returns one value for the whole cell; the kernel replaces the hard cell with a weighted
neighbourhood, and its local-linear version removes the first-order bias that binning
carries; SFI (stochastic force inference) projects the increments onto a global Fourier
basis, which is exactly the right basis for these fields. Each rung has one smoothing
parameter, chosen by cross-validation.

\textbf{Rung 4: learned smoothing, one dataset.} An MLP with periodic features, or a CNN
decoding a learned latent to an $M^d$ grid, fitted by least squares against $\dx/\dt$ on
one dataset. The network never sees $b$; the smoothing dial is the number of training
steps, chosen on held-out walkers. Because the noise is $10^3$ times the signal, the
training loss is uninformative and the only readable curve is the \emph{gain over
predicting zero}, $\overline{|y|^2}-\overline{|y-\hat b|^2}$, which on these data rises
from 0 to about $10^{-3}$ of the total target power.

\textbf{Rung 4b: amortised inference.} The same architecture trained once across 3000
simulated fields, each with its own short trajectory set. At inference it reads a new
dataset's binned increments and outputs a field with no retraining and no cross-validation.
The input is three physical channels \textemdash{} a z-scored mean increment
$Y/(\sigma\sqrt N)$, a shrunk mean $Y/(N+\lambda)$ and the shrinkage weight $N/(N+\lambda)$
with $\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2$ \textemdash{} so the network is handed the statistics rather
than the raw counts. A periodic U-Net with no normalisation layers (which would destroy the
physical amplitude) and $D_4$ dihedral test-time augmentation with proper vector-channel
transforms.

\textbf{Rung 5: score from static positions.} Estimating $\nabla\log\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}$ from
samples is exactly what training a diffusion model does, so denoising score matching here is
not an analogy: $\mathbb E\lVert\sigma s(x+\sigma\varepsilon,\sigma)+\varepsilon\rVert^2$
estimates the physical quantity directly. A Gaussian KDE differentiated in closed form is the
classical counterpart. Both select their smoothing by held-out Hyv\"arinen score matching,
$J(s)=\mathbb E[\tfrac12|s|^2+\nabla\!\cdot\!s]$, which ranks candidates by score error
without knowing the truth.

\section{Results}

All numbers below are on the same 2D random field and the same walkers unless stated.
Snapshot rows are a different kind of data on the same systems: independent positions
matched in count (capped at 500k), plus the known $D$, and no time ordering.

\subsection{More smoothing structure beats more flexibility \textemdash{} until the structure is wrong}

Ranked at 4.8M transitions the order is binning (0.293), kernel (0.126), network (0.100),
basis (0.097). Each step buys accuracy by assuming more, and the assumptions happen to be
true: the fields really are sums of Fourier modes, so SFI's global basis is not a
convenience but a match. The advantage disappears the moment the match does \textemdash{} on
fields built from other generators (sparse wells, cellular patterns) SFI drops behind the
local kernel, which assumes only smoothness.

The per-dataset network lands in the middle almost everywhere and wins where data is
scarcest (0.340 at 16 walkers). Its one structural weakness is that its smoothing dial is
noisy: with plenty of data the held-out curve is flat, and stopping on it costs about 5\%
of the error (0.082 against 0.076 at the truth-optimal step).

\subsection{Amortisation is the largest single win, and it is prior knowledge}

Trained once across 3000 fields, the same convolutional architecture that \emph{loses} to
an MLP on one dataset wins nearly every comparison: 0.060 against SFI's 0.097 at the
largest budget, 0.257 against 0.340 at the smallest, and 0.134 against 0.211 at high
diffusion. It needs no cross-validation and no retraining at inference.

What it has bought is a prior over fields, and the price is measurable. Its equivalent
smoothing kernel \textemdash{} the linear response $\partial\hat b(x_0)/\partial Y_c$ of
the output to each input cell \textemdash{} keeps a \emph{fixed width} as data grows and
instead raises its gain from 0.60 to 0.93. That is the signature of a Wiener filter matched
to the field statistics, not of a bandwidth being tuned. On fields from five other
generators it loses, by up to $1.9\times$.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{figs/learned_smoothing.png}
\caption{The amortised network's \emph{equivalent kernel}: the linear response of one
output cell to each input cell, recovered by differentiating the trained network. As the
data grows the kernel does not narrow \textemdash{} its width is set by the field prior
\textemdash{} it raises its gain from 0.60 to 0.93. A cross-validated bandwidth does the
opposite. This is the difference between learning a smoother and learning a prior.}
\end{figure}

\begin{keybox}
This is the cleanest result in the project for a reader deciding whether to amortise: the
learned estimator is better because it knows what the answer should look like, and it
degrades exactly as far as that knowledge is wrong. Both halves are measured, on the same
walkers, with the same score.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{At equilibrium, static positions beat increments}

This was the unexpected one. The Kramers\textendash Moyal target is dominated by noise of
size $\sqrt{2D/\dt}$; a \emph{position} is not noisy at all. On a gradient field, denoising
score matching on 500k independent snapshots reaches 0.072 where SFI on a matched number of
increments gets 0.097 \textemdash{} and using the very same walkers' positions rather than
idealised samples, it still beats every trajectory method at 16 and 64 walkers (0.302
against 0.392 and 0.358).

The gap widens with dimension. At $d=5$ with 6.1M transitions every trajectory method sits
near 0.93, i.e.\ barely better than predicting zero, while DSM on positions reaches
\textbf{0.625}. This is not a capacity limit \textemdash{} the same MLP fits the exact 5D
drift to 0.04 when handed noiseless targets \textemdash{} it is information: each increment
carries noise 48$\times$ the drift, and the field has ${\sim}1700$ independent correlation
volumes. With $100\times$ the data both MLP and SFI reach ${\sim}0.2$.

\subsection{Snapshots are blind to circulation, exactly and measurably}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/identifiability_report.png}
\caption{One field, $\omega$ swept from 0 to 4, which leaves $\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}$
bit-identical. Left: against the full drift the snapshot methods (dashed) climb towards 1
while trajectory methods improve. Middle: against the gradient part the snapshot methods are
flat. Right: against the rotational part their error is 1.00 everywhere \textemdash{} they
return no rotation at all.}
\end{figure}

Adding a rotational part leaves $\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}$ unchanged, so both snapshot estimators
return \emph{bit-identical} predictions for $\omega=0\ldots4$ (largest difference across the
five values: exactly 0.0). Their error against the rotational component is 1.00 at every
$\omega$, and against the full drift it climbs as $\omega/\sqrt{1+\omega^2}$, while
trajectory methods \emph{improve} with $\omega$ because a stronger rotation is more signal
against the same noise. The amortised CNN tracks $\omega$ up to 2 and fails at 4, outside
its training range of $|\omega|\le1.5$.

\subsection{Measurement noise separates the two families completely}

Position noise $\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}$ biases the increment target multiplicatively to
$b\,(1+\sigma^2/(D\dt))$ \textemdash{} the cell or neighbourhood is chosen with the noisy
position, so the estimator selects on the noise it then subtracts. At $\sigma=0.02$, one
percent of the box, that factor is 2.3, and every trajectory method scores above 1.2. On
snapshots the same noise merely convolves $\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}$ with a narrow Gaussian: the
KDE score goes $0.134\to0.140$, DSM $0.075\to0.080$.

The inflation formula is derived and verified term by term in the companion note
\code{docs/binned\_km\_error\_analysis.pdf}, where it is confirmed to 1.5\% over a factor of
5 in $\sigma$, and then against this project's own 2D sweeps to within 7\textendash10\%
across three values of $\dt$ with no fitted constant.

\subsection{The two families together measure dissipation}

The rotational part is what the two families disagree about:
$b_{\mathrm{rot}} = b - D\nabla\log\rho$. Its magnitude is the entropy production rate
$\sigma_{\mathrm{ep}}=\langle|b-D\nabla\log\rho|^2\rangle_\rho/D$, the dissipation that keeps
the system out of equilibrium. Taking the drift from the increments (SFI) and the score from
the positions \emph{the same walkers} visited (DSM):

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrrr@{}}
\toprule
$\omega$ & 0 & 0.5 & 1 & 2 & 4 \\
\midrule
exact & 0 & 0.924 & 3.695 & 14.78 & 59.12 \\
estimated & 0.160 & 1.109 & 3.763 & 14.79 & 58.91 \\
minus SFI's own predicted error & 0.093 & 0.992 & 3.671 & 14.69 & 58.79 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Within 2\% from $\omega\ge1$. Estimation error enters squared, so it always \emph{adds}: at
$\omega=0$ the estimate is 0.16 instead of 0, and subtracting SFI's own noise-level error
estimate \textemdash{} available without the truth \textemdash{} halves that floor. A
dissipation below ${\sim}0.1$ is not distinguishable from zero at this budget.

\subsection{Every method that tunes itself can be fooled by its own criterion}
\label{sec:selftuning}

Four separate instances, each caught only by comparing against the truth:
cross-validation chose a $10^6$-cell grid that answered for 10\% of the box (rung 1);
early stopping quit 30\% early on a flat held-out curve (rung 4); the KDE's bandwidth
search ran to the bottom of its grid on temporally correlated positions (1.06 against 0.35
at a fixed bandwidth) because it split samples at random rather than by walker; and the same
search ran to the \emph{top} of its grid at $D=1$, where the density is nearly flat and the
criterion has almost no signal to lock onto. Every rung therefore reports what it chose, not
only what it scored.

\section{The error budget, in closed form}

For rung 1 the whole error decomposes analytically and each term was verified against
simulation. For a cell of width $h$ holding $n$ increments,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MSE} \;\simeq\; \underbrace{\frac{2D}{n\dt}}_{\text{variance}}
  \;+\;\underbrace{\frac{|\nabla b|^2h^2}{12}}_{\text{readout bias}}
  \;+\;\underbrace{b^2\Big(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}^2}{D\dt}\Big)^{\!2}}_{\text{noise bias}},
  \qquad n \simeq M\rho h^d ,
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the total number of transitions. Substituting $n$ gives the three facts that
govern every rung:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=0.1em]
\item $\mathrm{Var}=2D/(M\rho h^d\dt)=2D/T_{\mathrm{occ}}$ depends only on the \emph{time}
      spent in the cell. Sampling faster makes each $\dx/\dt$ noisier and gives
      proportionally more of them: $\dt$ cancels.
\item Balancing against the bias gives $h^\star\propto M^{-1/(d+2)}$ and
      $\mathrm{MSE}^\star\propto M^{-2/(d+2)}$. To halve the error at $d=6$ needs
      $16\times$ the data. This is the honest reason the ladder continues past rung 1.
\item The measurement-noise term does \emph{not} lose its $\dt$, so it diverges as
      $\dt\to0$: with imperfect metrology, sampling faster is actively harmful.
\end{itemize}
Measured against 64 independent replicas: the variance law holds to within 1\textendash9\%
at every cell width and local density, the predicted optimal bin count is 17.0 against a
measured 16, and the noise inflation is confirmed to 1.5\%.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=0.15em]
\item \textbf{Comparisons across families are not like for like.} The amortised CNN knows the
      field distribution; the snapshot methods get independent samples and the true $D$.
      Both advantages are stated wherever their numbers appear, but they are advantages of
      the \emph{setting}, not of the estimator.
\item \textbf{One field family, one box.} Almost every number comes from Gaussian-spectrum
      fields on $[-1,1]^2$. The other-generator test is the only breadth check, and it is 2D.
\item \textbf{$D$ is known} to the snapshot methods, and $\dt$ is chosen by the simulator's
      stability rule rather than by an experiment.
\item \textbf{The snapshot estimators receive positions without walker labels}, so their
      cross-validation cannot group by walker the way the trajectory rungs do. That is a real
      defect in the correlated-positions setting, not only a caveat.
\item \textbf{Rung 4b is 2D-only} by construction, and its $64^2$ output grid caps its
      accuracy on nearly noise-free data.
\end{itemize}

% ==========================================================================
\section{What is worth doing next}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.15em]
\item \textbf{A hybrid estimator}: gradient part from positions, rotational remainder from
      increments. At 16 walkers it should beat both families; the pieces already exist.
\item \textbf{Estimate $D$ from the data} (quadratic variation) in rung 5 and report how the
      drift scale degrades. It is the one input the snapshot methods still get for free.
\item \textbf{Retrain the amortised CNN on a mixture of generators} and repeat the
      out-of-distribution test. It should trade in-family accuracy for robustness; how much
      is the interesting number.
\item \textbf{A calibrated posterior}: a conditional diffusion model over drift fields
      giving a distribution rather than a point estimate, with coverage checked against the
      true field. The data generation, scoring and entropy-production checks are already here.
\item \textbf{A time-resolution sweep}, which the error budget predicts should show the
      $O(\dt)$ discretisation bias trading against the $1/\dt$ noise amplification.
\item \textbf{Real data} \textemdash{} single-particle tracking or an ecological time series
      \textemdash{} which would expose what simulation hides: unknown $D$, non-stationary
      boundaries, heterogeneous noise.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Engineering decisions that changed the numbers}

A few implementation choices were not cosmetic, and each was forced by a measurement that
came out wrong first.

\textbf{An exact full-batch loss instead of minibatch SGD.} For the grid-based CNN the
least-squares objective over millions of increments collapses to a fixed set of lattice
statistics computed once,
$\sum_{c,o}G_o(c)\,b_c\!\cdot\!b_{c+o} - 2\sum_c b_c\!\cdot\!Y_c + \sum_i|y_i|^2$,
where $G$ and $Y$ are multilinear scatter sums of the data onto the lattice. The network
then trains against the \emph{exact} objective with no sampling noise, at a cost
independent of the dataset size. With a target whose noise is $10^3$ times the signal, the
gradient noise of minibatching is not a regulariser \textemdash{} it is the whole signal.
A test checks this loss against a float64 brute-force reference to $10^{-10}$.

\textbf{Sub-stepping the integrator.} Data are recorded at $\dt$ but integrated at
$\dt/\text{substeps}$. Otherwise the $O(\dt)$ discretisation bias of the estimator and the
$O(\dt)$ error of Euler\textendash Maruyama are the same number, and the ``bias'' being
plotted is the simulator's.

\textbf{One cache, many estimators.} Trajectories are keyed by a hash of the simulation
configuration, so every estimator is handed bit-identical walkers; sweep rows are cached by
the same mechanism, so re-running a study redraws its figures without recomputing anything.
Almost every claim in §4 is a paired comparison because of this, not an average over
independent runs.

\textbf{Symmetry as free data, done carefully.} The amortised network is trained with $D_4$
dihedral augmentation, but the channels are \emph{vectors}, and the box is a torus: a
reflection must transform the vector components as well as the pixels, and on a periodic
lattice it maps node $j\mapsto -j \bmod M$, i.e.\ a flip \emph{and} a roll by one. Getting
that wrong produces a network that is quietly worse rather than one that fails; a unit test
now asserts equivariance to all eight transforms.

\textbf{No normalisation layers.} A U-Net with batch or group normalisation learns to
produce fields of the right \emph{shape} and the wrong \emph{amplitude}, because the
physical scale of $b$ is exactly what normalisation removes. The scale is carried instead by
the input parameterisation: shrinkage weights built from an estimate of $\sigma^2=2D/\dt$
taken from the quadratic variation of the data itself.
