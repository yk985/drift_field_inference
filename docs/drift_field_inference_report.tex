{\large\color{accent} Six estimators, one set of walkers, and where each one wins}\\[0.8em]
{\large Youssef Kabbaj}\\[0.3em]
{\color{ink3}\small October 2026 \quad\textbullet\quad code: \code{github.com/yk985/drift\_field\_inference}}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.2em}\hrule height 0.4pt \vspace{0.8em}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Abstract.} We study the inverse problem of recovering the drift field $b$ of the
overdamped Langevin dynamics $\mathrm dx = b(x)\,\mathrm dt+\sqrt{2D}\,\mathrm dW$ from observed
particle motion. Over one recorded step the diffusive displacement is about 30 times the drift
displacement (about 1000 times in power), so every estimator must average away a much larger
random term without averaging away the field. We compare six estimators of increasing
structure \textemdash{} binning, kernel regression, basis projection, neural networks fitted
to one dataset, a network amortised over many simulated fields, and score-based methods that
use static positions only \textemdash{} on identical simulated walkers, so that differences
reflect the methods rather than the data.

We find that (i) the amortised network outperforms every classical estimator by roughly
25\textendash40\%, but loses to the simplest kernel on fields from generators it was not
trained on: its advantage is a learned prior. (ii) \emph{At equilibrium}, static positions
carry more information about the drift than the increments do. Each increment is dominated by
the diffusion term, of size $\sqrt{2D/\dt}$, whereas a position sample is free of it and, when
detailed balance holds, encodes the drift exactly through $b = D\nabla\log\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}$.
Denoising score matching on positions is the best per-dataset method in 2D and the only one
that recovers a 5D field. (iii) Away from equilibrium this advantage is lost on the rotational
part: static positions are \emph{exactly} blind to circulation, and every snapshot-based estimate
is unchanged to the last bit when a rotating component is added to the drift.
\end{keybox}

\section{The problem}

\subsection{The dynamics}

The overdamped Langevin equation
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm dx \;=\; b(x)\,\mathrm dt \;+\; \sqrt{2D}\,\mathrm dW
  \label{eq:sde}
\end{equation}
models a particle in a force field $b$ with thermal agitation of strength $D$ (the
diffusion coefficient). $W$ is a $d$-dimensional Wiener process (Brownian motion): its
increments over disjoint time intervals are independent and Gaussian, with
\begin{equation}
  \E[\mathrm dW_i] = 0,
  \qquad
  \E[\mathrm dW_i\,\mathrm dW_j] = \delta_{ij}\,\mathrm dt ,
  \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
  W(t+\dt)-W(t)\;\sim\;\mathcal N(0,\ \dt\, I_d).
\end{equation}
The noise term $\sqrt{2D}\,\mathrm dW$ therefore has zero mean and variance $2D\,\mathrm dt$
per component; for $b=0$ this is free diffusion with mean squared displacement $2dDt$.
Its standard deviation grows like $\sqrt{\mathrm dt}$, much faster than the drift's
$\mathrm dt$ for small $\mathrm dt$; this is the central difficulty of the problem.

The inverse problem, recovering $b$ from observed motion, arises in single-particle
tracking, active matter, ecology, and wherever a stochastic differential equation is fitted
to trajectories. We consider two kinds of data, which support fundamentally different answers.

\subsection{Trajectories: the drift is a conditional mean}

Positions recorded in time order at spacing $\dt$. Integrating \eqref{eq:sde} over one step,
$\dx = b(x)\dt + \sqrt{2D}\,\Delta W + O(\dt^{3/2})$, so
\begin{equation}
  \E[\dx \,|\, x] = b(x)\,\dt + O(\dt^2),
  \qquad
  \mathrm{Cov}[\dx \,|\, x] = 2D\dt\, I + O(\dt^2).
\end{equation}
This is the Kramers\textendash Moyal relation. It turns each recorded step into a noisy
sample of the field at the point the step started from:
\begin{equation}
  y_i \;\equiv\; \frac{\dx_i}{\dt} \;=\; b(x_i) + \eta_i ,
  \qquad
  \eta_i \sim \mathcal N\!\Big(0,\ \frac{2D}{\dt}\, I\Big).
  \label{eq:km}
\end{equation}
Estimating $b$ is therefore a regression of $y$ on $x$. The noise $\eta$ grows as $\dt$
shrinks: in the 2D settings used here ($D=0.3$, $\dt\approx10^{-3}$, RMS$|b|\approx1$) its
magnitude is 32 times that of the drift. The six estimators differ only in how they pool
neighbouring samples to reduce it.

\subsection{Snapshots: what the stationary density can and cannot tell}

Positions \emph{without} time order, drawn from the stationary density $\rss$. The density
obeys the Fokker\textendash Planck equation
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t \rho = -\nabla\!\cdot J,
  \qquad
  J = b\,\rho - D\nabla\rho ,
\end{equation}
where $J$ is the probability current. In the stationary state $\nabla\!\cdot J_{\mathrm{ss}}=0$.
Solving the definition of $J$ for $b$ gives an identity valid for \emph{any} drift:
\begin{equation}
  b \;=\; \underbrace{D\,\nabla\log\rss}_{\text{visible in snapshots}}
  \;+\; \underbrace{J_{\mathrm{ss}}/\rss}_{\text{invisible in snapshots}} .
  \label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
Snapshots determine $\rss$, hence the first term (the \emph{score} $\nabla\log\rss$, times
$D$). They say nothing about the second: any field $c$ with $\nabla\!\cdot(c\,\rss)=0$ can be
added to $b$ without changing $\rss$, so infinitely many drifts produce the same snapshots.
Only when the current vanishes \textemdash{} \emph{detailed balance}, i.e.\ thermodynamic
equilibrium \textemdash{} is $b = D\nabla\log\rss$ a pure gradient, fully determined by
positions alone.

\textbf{The fields used in this work make this exact.} We build every drift as
\begin{equation}
  b = -\nabla U + \omega\, A\nabla U ,
  \qquad A^\top = -A ,
  \label{eq:field}
\end{equation}
from one scalar potential $U$. Substituting $\rss = e^{-U/D}/Z$ into the current gives
$J_{\mathrm{ss}} = \omega\,\rss\, A\nabla U$, and its divergence vanishes term by term:
$\nabla\!\cdot(A\nabla U) = A_{ij}\partial_i\partial_j U = 0$ (an antisymmetric matrix
contracted with a symmetric Hessian), and $\nabla U\!\cdot\! A\nabla U = 0$ (an antisymmetric
quadratic form). So $\rss\propto e^{-U/D}$ for \emph{every} $\omega$: the rotational part
$\omega A\nabla U$ drives a steady circulating current $J_{\mathrm{ss}}\neq0$ yet leaves the
density untouched, and the score recovers only the gradient part,
\begin{equation}
  D\,\nabla\log\rss \;=\; -\nabla U \;=\; b_{\mathrm{grad}} .
  \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/helmholtz.png}
\caption{A drift field \eqref{eq:field} and its two parts. The rotational part (right) is
divergence-free and everywhere orthogonal to $\nabla\rss$, so $\rss$ is identical for every

great deal of it.}
\end{figure}

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{The identifiability gap is a property of the data, not of the estimators.} From
snapshots, only the gradient part of $b$ is recoverable, whatever the method; from
trajectories, all of $b$ is. We interpret all comparisons below with this limitation in mind.
\end{warnbox}

\section{Setup and measurement protocol}

\textbf{Fields.} $U$ is a Gaussian random field on the torus $[-1,1]^d$ with a tunable
correlation length, and $b$ is built from it by \eqref{eq:field}, so that $\omega$ controls the
circulation without changing $\rss$. Six other generators (sparse wells, cellular band-pass
patterns, Perlin noise, power-law spectra, terraces, an egg-carton lattice) are used only to
test generalisation.

\textbf{Simulation.} We integrate \eqref{eq:sde} with the Euler\textendash Maruyama scheme
and sub-stepping: the integrator runs at $\dt/\text{substeps}$ while positions are recorded at
$\dt$, so that an $O(\dt)$ bias of an estimator is not confused with an $O(\dt)$ error of the
integrator. Trajectories are cached by configuration, so every estimator is applied to
bit-identical walkers.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{figs/trajectories.png}
\caption{What the estimators are given: 2000 walkers $\times$ 4000 steps in the same field,
without and with circulation ($\omega=0$ and $1.5$), paths coloured by time. The drift is
invisible in any single step and appears only after averaging thousands of them. The
circulation on the right is recoverable from the increments and, by \eqref{eq:split},
absent from the positions.}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Error metric.} We score every estimate against the true field at $n=20\,000$
points $x_j$ drawn from $\rss$, i.e.\ where the walkers actually go and hence where the
estimate is used:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{nrmse}
  \;=\; \left(\frac{\sum_j |\hat b(x_j) - b(x_j)|^2}{\sum_j |b(x_j)|^2}\right)^{1/2} .
  \label{eq:nrmse}
\end{equation}
It is 0 for a perfect estimate and exactly 1 for the trivial estimate $\hat b\equiv0$; a
value of $0.10$ corresponds to an error of 10\% of the typical field magnitude, and values
near 1 indicate that essentially nothing has been learned.

\textbf{Tuning.} Each method selects its own smoothing parameter (bin size, bandwidth, basis
size, training length) by cross-validation grouped \emph{by walker} rather than by sample.
With a random split, a held-out point lies one time step away from its training neighbours,
and the criterion then favours a smoothing scale that is far too small (§\ref{sec:selftuning}).

\subsection{Methods 1\textendash4: least squares on the increments}

All four trajectory estimators minimise the same squared loss over different classes of
functions. We motivate this choice by three properties.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.15em]
\item \textbf{It targets $b$ without seeing $b$.} Since $\E[\eta\,|\,x]=0$, for any candidate
      $f$
      \begin{equation}
        \E\,|y - f(x)|^2 \;=\; \E\,|b(x)-f(x)|^2 \;+\; \E\,|\eta|^2 ,
        \label{eq:decomp}
      \end{equation}
      the error against the unknown field plus a constant. Minimising the loss on the
      observed $y$ minimises the error against $b$; its minimiser over all functions is the
      conditional mean $\E[y\,|\,x]=b(x)$.
\item \textbf{It is maximum likelihood.} The Euler transition density is Gaussian,
      $p(x_{k+1}|x_k)\propto\exp\!\big(-|\dx_k-b(x_k)\dt|^2/4D\dt\big)$, so the
      log-likelihood of a trajectory is $-\frac{\dt}{4D}\sum_k|y_k-b(x_k)|^2+\text{const}$.
\item \textbf{It yields an interpretable training curve.} Because the noise term in
      \eqref{eq:decomp} is about 1000 times the signal, the raw loss barely changes during
      training. We therefore monitor the \emph{gain over predicting zero},
      $L(0)-L(f) = \E|b|^2-\E|b-f|^2 = (1-\mathrm{nrmse}^2)\,\E|b|^2$, which isolates the part
      of the loss that depends on the estimate.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{lossbox}{Losses of methods 1\textendash4 (data $\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, $y_i=\dx_i/\dt$)}
\vspace{-0.6em}
\begin{align*}
\text{1. binning:}\quad
  & \hat b_c = \arg\min_{\beta}\ \sum_{i:\,x_i\in c}|y_i-\beta|^2
    \ =\ \text{mean of the $y_i$ in cell $c$} \\
\text{2. kernel (NW):}\quad
  & \hat b(x) = \arg\min_{\beta}\ \sum_i K_h(x_i-x)\,|y_i-\beta|^2 \\
\text{2. kernel (local-linear):}\quad
  & (\hat b(x),\hat B) = \arg\min_{\beta,B}\ \sum_i K_h(x_i-x)\,|y_i-\beta-B(x_i-x)|^2 \\
\text{3. SFI:}\quad
  & \hat c = \arg\min_{c}\ \sum_i \Big|y_i-\sum_{|k|\le K} c_k\,\phi_k(x_i)\Big|^2,
    \qquad \hat b=\textstyle\sum_k \hat c_k\phi_k \\
\text{4. neural network:}\quad
  & \hat\theta = \arg\min_{\theta}\ \frac1n\sum_i |y_i-f_\theta(x_i)|^2
\end{align*}
\vspace{-1.1em}

$K_h$ is a Gaussian kernel of bandwidth $h$; $\phi_k$ are Fourier modes on the torus. The
smoothing parameter of each \textemdash{} cell width, $h$, the number of modes $K$, the number
of training steps \textemdash{} is chosen by the same squared loss on held-out walkers.
\end{lossbox}

Binning returns one value for the whole cell; the local-linear kernel removes the
first-order bias this introduces; SFI (stochastic force inference) projects onto a global
Fourier basis, which is exactly the right basis for these fields. The networks are an MLP
with periodic input features, or a CNN that outputs the field on a grid. For the CNN, the
loss over millions of increments reduces exactly to a few lattice statistics, which allows
full-batch training without sampling noise.

\subsection{Method 4b: an amortised network, trained across fields}

The per-dataset network must be refitted for every new dataset. The amortised network is
instead trained \emph{once} on simulated data and then only applied.

\textbf{Training data.} 3000 fields drawn from the generator with randomised parameters
(correlation length 0.3\textendash0.7; $\omega=0$ for half, otherwise in $[-1.5,1.5]$; $D$
log-uniform in $[0.05,1.5]$; 256 or 1024 walkers for 1000\textendash4000 steps). Each
dataset is summarised on a $64\times64$ lattice by the count $N_c$ and the summed increments
$Y_c=\sum_{i\in c}y_i$ per cell.

\textbf{Inputs.} Five channels built from $N_c$, $Y_c$ and the noise level
$\sigma^2=2D/\dt$, which is estimated from the data itself: a z-score $Y/(\sigma\sqrt N)$, a
shrunk mean $Y/(N+\lambda)$, and the data weight $N/(N+\lambda)$, with $\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2$.
The shrunk mean is precisely the per-cell Bayesian estimate: if a cell's mean increment
$\bar Y=Y/N\sim\mathcal N(b,\sigma^2/N)$ and the prior is $b\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2)$, the
posterior mean is $Y/(N+\lambda)$. The network takes this estimate as input and learns the
spatial coupling between cells.

\begin{lossbox}{Loss of method 4b (over training fields $m$, on the $64^2$ lattice nodes $c$)}
\vspace{-0.4em}
\[
  L(\theta) \;=\; \E_m\left[\
  \frac{\sum_c w_c\,\big|f_\theta(X^{(m)})_c - b^{(m)}_c\big|^2}
       {\sum_c w_c\,\big|b^{(m)}_c\big|^2}\ \right],
  \qquad w_c = 0.8\,\rss(c)/\bar\rho + 0.2 .
\]
\vspace{-0.9em}

$X^{(m)}$ are the input channels of training dataset $m$ and $b^{(m)}$ its true field.
Unlike every other method, the truth appears in this loss \textemdash{} but only for the
simulated training fields. On a new dataset the network is applied without retraining
and never sees $b$.
\end{lossbox}

\textbf{Choice of loss.} The minimiser of $\E\,\|f(X)-b\|^2$ over all functions is the
posterior mean $\E[b\,|\,X]$ under the training distribution: the network is trained to be
the \emph{Bayes-optimal} estimator for fields drawn from its generator. As shown in
§\ref{sec:amort}, this property explains both its advantage and its failure mode. Dividing by
$\sum_c w_c|b_c|^2$ makes every field count equally regardless of its amplitude; weighting
by $\rss$ matches the evaluation metric \eqref{eq:nrmse}, with a 20\% floor so sparsely
visited regions are not ignored. The network is a periodic U-Net with no normalisation layers
(which would erase the physical amplitude), trained with the 8 symmetries of the square
lattice applied to inputs and outputs, vector components included.

\subsection{Method 5: the score, from static positions}

By \eqref{eq:score}, at equilibrium $b = D\,s$ with $s=\nabla\log\rss$. Estimating a score
from samples is precisely the task solved when training a diffusion model.

\begin{lossbox}{Losses of method 5 (data: positions $\{x_i\}$ drawn from $\rss$)}
\vspace{-0.4em}
\begin{align*}
\text{denoising score matching:}\quad
  & L(\theta) = \E_{x,\ \sigma,\ \varepsilon}\
    \big|\,\sigma\, s_\theta(x+\sigma\varepsilon,\ \sigma) + \varepsilon\,\big|^2 \\
  & \qquad x\sim\text{data},\quad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I),\quad
    \log\sigma\sim\mathcal U[\log 0.01,\ \log 0.5] \\
\text{KDE score:}\quad
  & s_h(x) = \nabla\log\hat\rho_h(x),
    \qquad \hat\rho_h(x)=\frac1n\sum_i \mathcal N(x;\,x_i,\,h^2 I) \\
\text{selection (both):}\quad
  & J(s) = \E_{x\sim\rss}\Big[\tfrac12|s(x)|^2 + \nabla\!\cdot s(x)\Big]
    \ \text{ on held-out positions}
\end{align*}
\vspace{-1.2em}
\end{lossbox}

\textbf{Choice of losses.} Denoising score matching is again least squares onto a
conditional mean. For a noised point $\tilde x = x+\sigma\varepsilon$, Tweedie's formula gives
$-\E[\varepsilon\,|\,\tilde x]/\sigma = \nabla\log\rho_\sigma(\tilde x)$, where
$\rho_\sigma=\rss*\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2 I)$; so the minimiser of $L$ is the score of a
slightly blurred density, and $\sigma$ plays the role of a bandwidth. The selection criterion
$J$ (Hyv\"arinen score matching) satisfies, after an integration by parts with no boundary
terms on the torus, $J(s)=\tfrac12\E|s-\nabla\log\rss|^2+\text{const}$: it ranks candidate
scores by their true error using samples alone. We use it to select the KDE bandwidth $h$ and
the noise level $\sigma$ at which the trained network is evaluated.

\subsection{More structure beats more flexibility \textemdash{} until the structure is wrong}

In the reference column the ranking is binning (0.293), kernel (0.126), network (0.100),
Fourier basis (0.097). Each method gains accuracy by assuming more, and here the assumptions
hold: these fields \emph{are} sums of Fourier modes, so the basis of SFI matches them exactly.
On fields from other generators (sparse wells, cellular patterns) SFI loses this advantage to
the kernel, which assumes only smoothness. The per-dataset network lies in between in almost
every setting and is the best of the per-dataset trajectory methods when data are scarcest
(0.340 at 16 walkers).

\subsection{The amortised network: a learned prior, its gain, and its price}
\label{sec:amort}

\textbf{In distribution.} On 100 held-out fields from the training generator, fitted at
three budgets (median 64k, 256k and 1M transitions), the network reaches a median nrmse of
0.213, 0.122 and 0.070, against 0.334, 0.192 and 0.089 for the better of SFI and the kernel;
it is the better method on 99\%, 97\% and 93\% of the fields. On the reference walkers it
reaches 0.060 against 0.097 for SFI, without cross-validation and in 0.6 seconds.

\textbf{What the network learns: a filter, not a bandwidth.} To characterise the learned
estimator we linearise it. Its output at a node $c_0$ responds to the mean increment
$\bar Y_c=Y_c/N_c$ of every cell $c$ through the \emph{equivalent kernel}
\begin{equation}
  K_c = \frac{\partial \hat b(c_0)}{\partial \bar Y_c},
  \qquad
  \hat b(c_0) \approx \sum_c K_c\,\bar Y_c ,
\end{equation}
computed exactly by automatic differentiation. We summarise it by two numbers: its
\emph{width} (the RMS radius of $|K|$) and its \emph{gain} $G=\sum_c K_c$, the fraction of a
uniform drift that is transmitted. A classical smoother has $G=1$ by construction (its weights
sum to one) and adapts by changing its width: as the data grow, the cross-validated kernel
bandwidth roughly halves, from about 0.17 to 0.08. The network behaves in the opposite way:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{\qquad}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
walkers & 16 & 64 & 256 & 1024 & $D$ & 0.05 & 0.15 & 0.4 & 1.0 \\
\midrule
width & 0.260 & 0.259 & 0.237 & 0.262 & width & 0.119 & 0.177 & 0.256 & 0.337 \\
gain $G$ & 0.60 & 0.77 & 0.92 & 0.93 & gain $G$ & 0.79 & 0.93 & 0.76 & 0.39 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

As the budget grows 64-fold, the width barely changes while the gain rises from 0.60 to
0.93. As the noise grows, the kernel both widens \emph{and} shrinks more strongly, down to
$G=0.39$ at $D=1$.

\textbf{Why shrinkage is optimal.} A smoother with unit gain is unbiased but transmits all
the noise it averages; multiplying the estimate by $G<1$ introduces a bias $(1-G)\,b$ and
reduces the variance by a factor $G^2$. When noise dominates, this trade-off is strongly
favourable, and the optimal amount of shrinkage depends on the typical magnitude of the
fields, that is, on the prior. In the idealised linear-Gaussian case the Bayes-optimal estimator is the Wiener filter,
which acts on each Fourier mode $k$ of the data separately,
\begin{equation}
  \hat b(k) \;=\; \frac{S(k)}{S(k)+\mathcal N(k)}\;\bar Y(k),
\end{equation}
with $S$ the power spectrum of the field prior and $\mathcal N\propto\sigma^2/n$ the noise
power per mode. More data lowers $\mathcal N$ and pushes the factor towards 1, so the gain
rises; more noise raises $\mathcal N$ and suppresses the high-$k$ modes first, so the filter
widens and shrinks. The network is neither linear nor exactly this filter, but it exhibits the
same signature. Its kernels are moreover elongated with negative side lobes: the
$x$-component of a gradient field is correlated further along $y$ than along $x$, and the
network has learned this anisotropic covariance, which no isotropic bandwidth can represent.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figs/learned_smoothing.png}
\caption{The network's equivalent kernel $K_c$ on the reference field (never seen in
training). Top: walkers from 16 to 1024 at $D=0.3$. Bottom: $D$ from 0.05 to 1 at 400
walkers. Dashed circles: twice the bandwidth that cross-validation picks for the
local-linear kernel on the same data. Right: width against budget and against noise for both.}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The cost of a prior.} The above relies on the training generator being a good model
of the field. When it is not, the bias $(1-G)\,b$, previously cheap, becomes the dominant
error, because the network shrinks the features that its prior considers unlikely. We test
this on six generators not seen during training (eight fields each, 1M transitions, same
amplitude):

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrrrr@{}}
\toprule
median nrmse & egg carton & terraces & power-law & Perlin & band-pass & sparse wells \\
\midrule
local-linear kernel & 0.283 & \textbf{0.602} & \textbf{0.612} & \textbf{0.642} & 0.442 & 0.375 \\
SFI & 0.163 & 0.619 & 0.729 & 0.743 & \textbf{0.379} & \textbf{0.356} \\
amortised CNN & \textbf{0.151} & 0.702 & 0.726 & 0.803 & 0.721 & 0.683 \\
\emph{amortised / best} & \emph{0.92} & \emph{1.17} & \emph{1.19} & \emph{1.25} & \emph{1.90} & \emph{1.92} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The network wins only on the egg carton, a smooth single-scale field that resembles a
long-correlation training field, and it performs worst precisely where the classical methods
do well. A band-pass field concentrates its power on a ring of wavenumbers that the Gaussian
prior considers improbable at that amplitude, so these modes are damped. Sparse wells
\textemdash{} a plain of zero drift broken by narrow wells with drift up to $4\times$ the RMS
\textemdash{} are treated as noise: smoothed away and pulled back towards familiar amplitudes.
Shifting a single parameter beyond the training range follows the same rule: a rotation
$\omega=3$ (trained on $|\omega|\le1.5$) costs a factor $3.3$, a drift twice as strong $2.2$,
near-noise-free data ($D=0.02$) $3.5$; rougher fields, smoother fields and much noisier data,
which the prior does not exclude, are handled as well as or better than by the classical
methods. SFI and the kernel have no training distribution to depart from; their errors
change only with the difficulty of the problem.

\begin{keybox}
The amortised network outperforms the classical estimators because it encodes what the
answer should look like, and it degrades exactly as far as this knowledge is wrong. It is
therefore appropriate only when the training generator is a credible model of the system;
otherwise it should be trained on a broader mixture of generators.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{At equilibrium, static positions beat increments, increasingly with dimension}

\textbf{The role of the diffusion term.} An increment and a position carry the drift in very
different ways. An increment measures $b$ directly, but through \eqref{eq:km}: every sample
is buried under the diffusion term $\eta$, of standard deviation $\sqrt{2D/\dt}$, which is
32 times the drift here and grows without bound as the sampling interval shrinks. A static
position carries no such term. \emph{At equilibrium}, i.e.\ when detailed balance holds and
$J_{\mathrm{ss}}=0$, the stationary density encodes the drift exactly through
$b=D\nabla\log\rss$ \eqref{eq:split}, so the same physical information can be read from where
the particles are rather than from how they move, without paying the diffusive noise of each
step. The comparison below is restricted to gradient fields ($\omega=0$) for this reason.

On such a field, denoising score matching on 500k independent snapshots reaches 0.072 in 2D,
where the best per-dataset trajectory method on 4.8M increments reaches 0.097. Using the
walkers' own (time-correlated) positions instead of idealised snapshots, it still outperforms
every per-dataset trajectory method at 16 and 64 walkers (0.302 and 0.164, against 0.340 and
0.173 for the best of them). The advantage grows with dimension:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{\qquad}lrrrr@{}}

\midrule

local-linear & 0.116 & 0.261 & 0.925 & 1.004 & DSM & \textbf{0.072} & \textbf{0.142} & \textbf{0.625} & 0.996 \\

\end{center}

At $d=5$ every trajectory method remains near 0.93, barely better than predicting zero,
whereas denoising score matching reaches 0.625 from twelve times fewer samples. We identify
three reasons.

\textbf{Increments lose information as $d$ grows; positions do not.} Information about $b$
accumulates with observed \emph{time}, not with the number of steps: the variance of a local
estimate is $2D/T_{\mathrm{occ}}$, with $T_{\mathrm{occ}}$ the time spent in the region (§5).
A stable simulation needs $\dt$ to shrink with $d$ (the diffusive step $\sqrt{2dD\dt}$ must
stay small against the field's length scale): $\dt=1.05\times10^{-3}$ in 2D but
$2.7\times10^{-4}$ in 5D. The same 6.1M transitions therefore cover four times less time,
and the per-step noise grows from 32 to 109 times the drift. Meanwhile the field has
${\sim}1700$ independent correlation volumes in 5D. The 1600 time units of data leave about
one unit per volume, i.e.\ a standard deviation $\sqrt{2D}\approx0.8$ per component against a
signal of 0.44: the signal-to-noise ratio is below one everywhere, and every trajectory
method shrinks towards zero. The limitation is one of information rather than capacity: the
same MLP fits the exact 5D drift to 0.04 when given noiseless targets.

\textbf{A snapshot is a full, independent sample.} Each position is a draw from $\rss$, whose
log-gradient \emph{is} the drift divided by $D$; its information does not depend on $\dt$.
500k snapshots provide ${\sim}300$ independent samples per correlation volume, and the noise
in the score regression is the level $\sigma$ chosen by the method, not the diffusive noise
$\sqrt{2D/\dt}$ imposed by the physics.

\textbf{A shared model, not a local average.} The KDE score is a fixed-bandwidth local average
whose gradient variance grows like $h^{-(d+2)}$, the same curse of dimensionality as binning,
and it fails in 5D (0.983). Denoising score matching fits one neural network across the whole
space and shares statistical strength between regions. At $d=10$, 500k snapshots are no
longer sufficient for either.

We stress that this advantage holds only at equilibrium: it relies on $J_{\mathrm{ss}}=0$ in
\eqref{eq:split}, and it disappears for the rotational part of the drift, as shown next.

\subsection{Snapshots are blind to circulation, exactly}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/identifiability_report.png}

\end{figure}

As \eqref{eq:split} predicts, both snapshot estimators return \emph{bit-identical}
predictions for $\omega=0,\dots,4$ (largest difference across the five values: exactly 0.0).
Their error against the rotational component is 1.00 at every $\omega$, and against the full
drift it increases as $\omega/\sqrt{1+\omega^2}$, the fraction of $|b|$ that is rotational.
Trajectory methods instead \emph{improve} with $\omega$, since a stronger rotation means more
signal against the same noise. The amortised network tracks $\omega$ up to 2 and fails at 4,
outside its training range.

\subsection{Position measurement error: a bias for trajectories, almost nothing for snapshots}

In experiments, positions are measured with error, for instance the localisation error of a
microscope. We model it as $\tilde x_k = x_k+\varepsilon_k$ with independent
$\varepsilon_k\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2 I)$. The two families respond very differently.

\textbf{Trajectories.} The measured increment is
$\Delta\tilde x_k = \dx_k + \varepsilon_{k+1} - \varepsilon_k$. This adds a variance
$2\sigma^2$, which averages out like any other noise, but also a \emph{bias}, which does not.
Every trajectory method groups increments by their measured starting point $\tilde x_k$, and
the same $\varepsilon_k$ that displaced this measurement enters the increment with the
opposite sign. Suppose that $\rss$ increases to the right. A particle measured at $\tilde x$
is then more likely to lie to its right, where there are more particles, so the error
$\varepsilon_k=\tilde x_k-x_k$ tends to point left; precisely, for small $\sigma$ (Tweedie's
formula),
\begin{equation}
  \E[\varepsilon_k \,|\, \tilde x_k] \;=\; -\sigma^2\,\nabla\log\rss(\tilde x_k).
\end{equation}
The increment carries $-\varepsilon_k$, which therefore points right, towards higher density
\textemdash{} along $b$. Dividing by $\dt$ and using $\nabla\log\rss=b/D$:
\begin{equation}
  \E\Big[\frac{\Delta\tilde x_k}{\dt}\,\Big|\,\tilde x_k\Big]
  \;\approx\; b + \frac{\sigma^2}{\dt}\,\nabla\log\rss
  \;=\; b\,\Big(1+\frac{\sigma^2}{D\dt}\Big).
\end{equation}
Every trajectory method therefore estimates a field that is too large by this factor,
regardless of the amount of data. The ratio compares the measurement error $\sigma$ with the
size of one diffusive step, $\sqrt{2D\dt}\approx0.025$: at $\sigma=0.02$, one percent of the
box, the two are comparable and the estimate is $2.3$ times too large. Recording less often
(larger $\dt$) reduces the bias; more data does not.

\textbf{Snapshots.} Noisy positions are exact samples of the blurred density
$\rss*\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2I)$, whose score differs from the true one by a relative amount of
order $(\sigma/\ell)^2\approx10^{-2}$, with $\ell\approx0.24$ the length scale of the field.

\subsection{Self-tuning methods can be misled by their own criterion}
\label{sec:selftuning}

We observed four such failures, each detectable only by comparison with the true field.
Cross-validation selected a $10^6$-cell grid that answered for 10\% of the box (binning).
Early stopping ended training 30\% too early on a flat held-out curve (MLP). The KDE bandwidth
search ran to the bottom of its grid on temporally correlated positions (1.06 against 0.35 at
a fixed bandwidth), because it split samples at random rather than by walker, and to the
\emph{top} of its grid at $D=1$, where the density is nearly flat and the criterion carries
almost no signal (the 0.843 in Table~\ref{tab:main}). For this reason we report the selected
hyperparameters alongside every score.

% ==========================================================================
\section{The error budget, in closed form}

For binning, the error decomposes analytically, and we verified each term against
simulation. For a cell of width $h$ holding $n$ increments,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MSE} \;\simeq\; \underbrace{\frac{2D}{n\dt}}_{\text{variance}}
  \;+\;\underbrace{\frac{|\nabla b|^2h^2}{12}}_{\text{bias: one value per cell}}
  \;+\;\underbrace{b^2\Big(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}^2}{D\dt}\Big)^{\!2}}_{\text{bias: position error}},
  \qquad n \simeq M\rss h^d ,
\end{equation}
with $M$ the total number of transitions. Substituting $n$ yields three properties shared by
all trajectory methods:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=0.1em]
\item $\mathrm{Var}=2D/(M\rss h^d\dt)=2D/T_{\mathrm{occ}}$ depends only on the \emph{time}
      spent in the cell: sampling faster makes each $\dx/\dt$ noisier but provides
      proportionally more of them, so $\dt$ cancels.
\item Balancing variance against bias gives $h^\star\propto M^{-1/(d+2)}$ and
      $\mathrm{MSE}^\star\propto M^{-2/(d+2)}$: halving the error at $d=6$ requires $16$ times
      more data. This motivates the more structured estimators.
\item The measurement-error term retains its $1/\dt$, so with imperfect measurements faster
      sampling is harmful.
\end{itemize}
Over 64 independent simulated replicas, the variance law holds to within
1\textendash9\% at every cell width and local density, the predicted optimal bin count is 17.0
against a measured 16, and the inflation factor is confirmed to within 1.5\%.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=0.12em]
\item \textbf{The families are not compared like for like.} The amortised network knows the
      field distribution; the snapshot methods get independent samples and the true $D$.
\item \textbf{One field family, one box.} Almost every number comes from Gaussian-spectrum
      fields on $[-1,1]^2$; the other-generator test is the only breadth check, and it is 2D.
\item \textbf{$D$ is known} to the snapshot methods, and $\dt$ is set by the simulator's
      stability rule rather than by an experiment.
\item \textbf{The snapshot estimators receive positions without walker labels}, so their
      cross-validation cannot group by walker, which is a genuine weakness when positions are
      correlated in time.
\item \textbf{The amortised network is 2D-only}, and its $64^2$ output grid caps its accuracy
      on nearly noise-free data.
\end{itemize}

\section{Outlook}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.12em]
\item \textbf{Time-dependent fields.} All fields studied here are stationary. A drift
      $b(x,t)$ that changes in time removes the stationary density altogether, so the snapshot
      methods lose their foundation and the trajectory methods must pool increments over
      finite time windows, trading temporal resolution against noise. Characterising this
      trade-off, and extending the amortised network to space\textendash time inputs, is the
      most natural next step.
\item \textbf{Other models.} The architectures used here are deliberately simple. Score
      networks parameterised as the gradient of a scalar ($s=-\nabla\phi$) would enforce the
      curl-free structure of equilibrium drifts; equivariant or attention-based networks
      operating directly on trajectories, rather than on binned statistics, would remove the
      $64^2$ grid and the restriction to 2D; and conditioning on $D$ would allow a single
      amortised model to cover a wider range of noise levels.
\item \textbf{A hybrid estimator}: the gradient part from positions and the rotational
      remainder from increments, which should outperform both families with few walkers.
\item \textbf{Estimating $D$ from the data} (quadratic variation) in the snapshot methods,
      and quantifying how the drift scale degrades.
\item \textbf{Training the amortised network on a mixture of generators}, to measure how much
      in-family accuracy is traded for robustness.
\item \textbf{A calibrated posterior}: a conditional diffusion model over drift fields giving
      a distribution rather than a point estimate, with coverage checked against the truth.
\item \textbf{Real data}, such as single-particle tracking or ecological time series, with
      unknown $D$, non-stationary boundaries and heterogeneous noise.
\end{enumerate}
